Listen, my children, gather close by the fire, and let me tell you a tale of pride and patience, of a heart once broken and then, like the morning sun, made whole again.

Long ago, in the gentle hills of eMakhosi, stood the Great Place of KwaMabena. Its owner was Inkosi Vusi Mabena, a man whose love for his own reflection surpassed his love for a good harvest. He carried no book, save the great scroll of his ancestors, which he would unfurl and recite, finding comfort in the names of chiefs and warriors long past. Oh, how his chest would swell with pride as he traced the lineage of the Mabenas, from the first warrior to his own esteemed self! Any worry, any slight from the world, would fade as he chanted the praises of his forefathers, forgetting the empty grain stores and the dwindling cattle. He would always begin with his own entry: "Vusi Mabena, born in the season of the first rains, married MaMabena, daughter of the wise Mkhize clan. She bore him Nomusa, Naledi, and Ntombi, and a son who returned to the ancestors too soon." He had even added the day his beloved wife joined the spirit world, and how Ntombi had married Charles, son of the Ngubane clan of KwaNgubane. For Inkosi Vusi, his own face in the morning dew, and the sound of his clan name echoing in the valleys, were the two greatest blessings a man could possess.

His wife, MaMabena the Elder, now with the ancestors, had been a woman of deep wisdom and gentle spirit. For seventeen years, she had smoothed the rough edges of his towering pride, a silent pillar of strength. She had nurtured her three daughters: Nomusa, as proud and striking as her father; Ntombi, who found fault in every shadow; and Naledi, whose quiet grace and thoughtful eyes often went unseen by her own kin, like a rare flower blooming in the shade. MaMabena had left her daughters to the guidance of a conceited father, but she had entrusted Naledi's spirit to her dear friend, MaDlamini, a woman whose counsel was as clear as mountain water.

***

Many seasons later, Ntombi, now MaNgubane, lay weak in a small dwelling by the great waters of eLwandle, recovering from a fever. She grumbled often, as was her way, about the simple meals and the lack of attention, yet she found much to enjoy in the bustling coastal town. Her husband, Charles Ngubane, a good-hearted man, and Naledi, her quiet sister, were by her side.

One day, Naledi asked after Themba, the man of the great waters, a brave captain who had been visiting eLwandle. Ntombi's face clouded like the sky before a storm. Charles chuckled. "Oh, Themba is well, I suppose, but he is a strange one. We invited him to KwaNgubane, and he seemed delighted. But then, he made a flimsy excuse, saying he didn't hunt and had been misunderstood. It seems he feared our home would be too dull for his broken heart."

Charles then turned to Naledi, his eyes twinkling. "No, Ntombi, you know the truth! He imagined that if he came, he would find *you* there, Naledi! He thought everyone lived at KwaNgubane. When he learned MaDlamini's kraal was a journey away, his spirit faltered. He had not the courage to come. It is true, Ntombi knows it." But Ntombi would not graciously admit this, perhaps unwilling to concede that Naledi's presence was a greater draw than her own. Yet, Naledi's kind heart swelled with a quiet warmth. She continued her enquiries. "He speaks of you, Naledi," Charles insisted, "in such terms—" Ntombi interrupted, "Charles, I never heard him speak of Naledi twice the whole time! He never speaks of you at all, Naledi!" Charles conceded, "Perhaps not in general talk, but it is clear his admiration for you is as deep as the ocean. His head is filled with books you recommended; he yearns to speak to you of them."

***

Inkosi Vusi and Nomusa were deeply stung by this betrayal, for they had relied on MaSithole’s flattery. They sought comfort in their distant, proud cousins, but soon learned that to flatter others without being flattered in return was like eating a meal with no salt.

Naledi, however, found her path bathed in the sunshine of Themba’s love. Her only sorrow was that she had no grand family, no kraal full of respected elders, no lineage as strong as a baobab tree, to offer him in return for his own noble kin. She felt this lack keenly. The difference in their worldly wealth was a mere pebble in the river; it caused her no regret. But to have no family to receive and honour him, no spirit of respect or goodwill to offer for the warmth and welcome she found in his brothers and sisters—this was a pain as sharp as a thorn. She had only MaDlamini and MaSmith, a friend whose quiet wisdom had recently helped her, to add to his circle. Yet Themba, with his generous heart, welcomed them both. He now valued MaDlamini, despite her past mistakes, and MaSmith, with her many kindnesses, quickly became a cherished friend.

Indeed, Themba proved himself a true warrior, helping MaSmith reclaim her husband’s lost cattle and land in distant lands, acting with the strength and resolve of a fearless man. MaSmith’s joy grew, not just from her improved fortunes and health, but from the gift of such friends. Her cheerfulness was a spring that never ran dry, just as Naledi’s happiness flowed from the warmth of her own heart. Naledi was tenderness itself, and she found its full worth in Themba’s deep affection. His life on the great waters was the only thing that gave her friends pause, for the threat of distant wars could dim her sunshine. She gloried in being the wife of a man of the sea, yet she bore the silent tax of quick alarm for belonging to a calling honoured as much for its quiet strength at home as for its power in the world.

And so, the quiet flower, Naledi, found her place, her heart filled, and her spirit at peace, like a calm river flowing to the boundless sea.
