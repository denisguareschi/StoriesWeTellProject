Lord Arata Masayuki, master of the revered Arata-tei in Izu Province, found his deepest pleasure not in the beauty of his meticulously sculpted gardens nor the wisdom gleaned from ancient scrolls, but in the "Meika Keifu no Maki," the Scroll of Illustrious Houses. There, amidst the elegant brushstrokes depicting his ancestors' noble deeds and the precise records of their lineage, he would spend countless hours. In moments of ease, it filled him with serene pride; in times of vexation, it offered solace, transforming his earthly troubles into a detached pity for lesser houses. His own entry, meticulously updated in his elegant hand, was always the first he sought: "Arata Masayuki, born in the year of the Boar, married Lady Ayame, daughter of the esteemed Fujiwara clan, who departed in the year of the Dragon..." He would add the date of his wife's passing, and later, Mari-san's marriage to the Musashi heir. Lord Arata, at fifty-four, still possessed a noble visage, and his vanity was as boundless as the ocean. He believed a distinguished lineage and a comely appearance were the greatest gifts bestowed by the heavens. His late wife, Okusama Arata, had been a woman of profound wisdom and gentle spirit, a tranquil pond reflecting the moon, whose steadfastness had long maintained the family's honor. She had left behind three daughters: Etsuko-hime, Akane-hime, and Mari-san, a heavy legacy for a father so consumed by his own reflection. Fortunately, her dearest friend, Okusama Kikuko, a woman of sensible heart, resided nearby in the village, offering guidance and a steady hand.

Months later, in Minato-cho, the bustling harbor town, Mari-san, now of the Musashi family, was recovering from a minor ailment. Though her complaints were many, her spirit found much to enjoy amidst the sea air and new faces. Yet, she fretted over Kaigun Taisho Hayato, a melancholy naval captain, who often visited their lodgings. "He is quite peculiar," she declared, "He promised to join us at Ueno-mura for a hunt, then offered the strangest excuses, claiming he 'never hunted' and was 'misunderstood.' I believe he simply feared dullness, though our cottage is lively enough for one so heart-broken." Her husband, Charles Musashi, merely chuckled. "No, Mari-san, you know the truth. It was Akane-hime he sought. He imagined she lived close by and, discovering Okusama Kikuko’s residence was a distant three *ri* from Ueno-mura, his courage failed him." Mari-san, whether from a desire to deny Akane-hime's allure or deeming the captain unworthy of an Arata, did not accept this gracefully. Akane-hime, however, felt a quiet blush of flattery. "He speaks of you constantly," Charles added, "His mind is filled with the ancient scrolls you recommended, and he wishes to discuss them with you."

Kageyama Shinjiro, whose ambition was as sharp as a newly forged blade, soon departed Onsen-kyo, the hot spring capital. When Kiyo-san, his companion, was later heard to be under his protection in the capital, it became clear his double-hearted game. Lord Arata and Etsuko-hime were mortified, their pride shattered by the deception. They had leaned on Kiyo-san's constant flattery, only to find themselves unadorned and exposed. Akane-hime, however, found her true happiness. Her heart, once a quiet stream, now flowed with the boundless joy of Kaigun Taisho Kenshin's affection. Her only sorrow was the lack of a grand lineage or vast estates to offer him, for his noble spirit deserved the highest honor. The disparity in their fortunes mattered not a whit, but to have no distinguished family to welcome and esteem him, no honored connections to match his own, caused her a pang of quiet pain. Yet, Kenshin-dono cherished her two friends, Okusama Kikuko, whose past misjudgments he now forgave, and O-haru-san, whose kind deeds had secured their marriage. Kenshin-dono, with the steadfastness of a fearless warrior, helped O-haru-san recover her husband’s lost properties. Akane-hime, in turn, found her spring of felicity in her spirit's warmth, much like her friend O-haru-san. She gloried in being a sailor's wife, though the shadow of war, ever present on the horizon, was the only cloud that could dim her sunshine.
