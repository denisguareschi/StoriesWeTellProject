Kwasukasukela, in the time when the animals spoke and the land breathed with ancient spirits, along the winding banks of the great Limpopo river, on a day when the sun beat down like a blacksmith's hammer, lived Ntshimmiri the Monkey, with his bright, mischievous eyes, and Kgalagala the Crab, with his scuttling legs and clever, snapping claws.

One morning, as they played by the water's edge, Kgalagala unearthed a plump, juicy marula fruit, sweet and fragrant. "Look, Ntshimmiri!" he scuttled, holding it high. "A treasure, indeed!" Ntshimmiri, ever restless, then found a hard, dry baobab seed. "And I, Kgalagala, have found something even greater!" he chattered, though he knew the seed was as useless as a pebble for eating.

Ntshimmiri's greedy heart yearned for Kgalagala's sweet marula. "Ah, but you are short-sighted, my friend," he began, his voice dripping with honey. "This marula, you eat today, and then it is gone. But my baobab seed! Plant it, and in time, it will grow into a mighty tree, reaching for the heavens, bearing many fruits year after year. Imagine! If you don't believe me, I will plant it myself, and you will surely regret your foolishness." Kgalagala, simple-hearted and easily swayed by the vision of a great tree, agreed. Ntshimmiri snatched the marula, gobbling it down, and reluctantly gave the hard baobab seed to Kgalagala, fearing the crab's sharp pinch if he refused. They parted ways, Ntshimmiri returning to his acacia tree, Kgalagala to his home amongst the river stones.

Kgalagala planted the seed. The seasons turned, the rains came, the sun shone, and slowly, slowly, a tiny shoot pushed through the red earth. It grew, year by year, until it became a strong *umkhiwane* tree, laden with ripe, sweet figs, glowing like embers in the late afternoon sun. Kgalagala would stretch his stalked eyes, watching them ripen, his mouth watering. "Oh, how delicious they will be!" he whispered to himself.

But Kgalagala, with his short, ground-hugging legs, could not climb the smooth trunk. He tried, and tried, but his efforts were in vain. In his despair, he remembered Ntshimmiri, the cleverest climber in all the bushveld. He scuttled off, over the stones and through the tall grasses, until he found Ntshimmiri napping in a Marula tree, his long tail wrapped around a branch.

Ntshimmiri awoke, his eyes gleaming when he heard of the mighty *umkhiwane* tree laden with fruit. A wicked plan hatched in his mind. He would climb the tree, eat all the ripe figs, and throw the hard, unripe ones at Kgalagala. And so he did. He picked the sweetest figs, stuffing his cheeks, and hurled the green, bitter ones at poor Kgalagala, who stood below, defenceless. *Thump! Thump!* they struck, until Kgalagala lay still, his shell cracked, his life gone, beneath the tree he had nurtured. Ntshimmiri, seeing his evil work, fled like a coward into the shadows of the bush.

Later, Kgalagala's young son, Mfana-Kgalagala, came upon his father's broken body, surrounded by the bitter green figs. His heart broke, and he wept until his tears dried. "No," he resolved, "this crying achieves nothing. I must avenge my father!" He looked up at the tree, saw the missing ripe fruit, the scattered peels, and knew instantly who the murderer was. His father had told him of the marula and the baobab seed. Ntshimmiri, with his insatiable greed, was the killer.

Mfana-Kgalagala knew he couldn't face Ntshimmiri alone. He sought out his father's old friends: Imbokodo, the Grinding Stone; Nyosi, the Bee; and Ilahle, the Live Coal. He told them his sorrowful tale. Imbokodo rumbled, Nyosi buzzed fiercely, and Ilahle glowed red with anger. "We will help you, little one," they vowed. "The wicked must always face justice."

Ntshimmiri, meanwhile, thought himself clever, safe in his hut behind the kraal. "No one saw me," he chattered, "dead crabs tell no tales!" But his boredom soon got the better of him. Then, a messenger arrived, a small field mouse, bowing low. "Great Ntshimmiri," he squeaked, "my master, Mfana-Kgalagala, invites you to a memorial feast for his late father. He remembers your friendship and wishes you to honour his home." Ntshimmiri's heart swelled with false pride. "Ah, my old friend," he feigned, wiping a dry eye. "I shall certainly attend."

He arrived, full of false smiles, and was led to the hut. After a grand meal, Mfana-Kgalagala led him to a quiet corner where a small cooking fire glowed. "A moment of reflection, friend," he said, then slipped away. Ntshimmiri, thirsty and impatient, reached for the water pot near the fire. Suddenly, *POP!* Ilahle, the Live Coal, burst from the ashes and burned Ntshimmiri's neck! He shrieked and leaped back. As he ran for the door, Nyosi, the Bee, flew from behind the woven mat and stung him fiercely on the cheek! Ntshimmiri shrieked again, dashing out, but as he passed beneath the kraal gate, *CRASH!* Imbokodo, the Grinding Stone, fell from above, landing squarely on his head! Ntshimmiri lay crushed, unable to move. Mfana-Kgalagala scuttled forward, his sharp claws raised. "Do you remember my father now, you murderer?" he hissed. "It was... your father's... fault!" gasped Ntshimmiri, still unrepentant. "Still lying?" cried Mfana-Kgalagala, and with a swift, sharp snap, he ended Ntshimmiri's wicked life. Justice had been served.
