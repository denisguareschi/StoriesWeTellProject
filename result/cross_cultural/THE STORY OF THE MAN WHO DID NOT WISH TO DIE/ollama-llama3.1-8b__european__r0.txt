The Tale of the Man Who Would Not Die

In the quaint village of Ashwood, nestled in the rolling hills of England, there lived a gentleman named Edward Fanshawe. His family had been well-to-do for generations, and Edward, though not excessively rich, was by no means poor. He had inherited a considerable sum from his father, which he spent with a carelessness that bordered on extravagance, until he reached the age of thirty-two.

One day, without warning, the specter of mortality crept into Edward's thoughts. The prospect of falling ill or dying filled him with a sense of wretchedness. "I wish to live," he exclaimed to himself, "until I am at least six hundred years old, free from all sickness and pain." He pondered the possibility of prolonging his life through simple living and frugality, recalling tales of ancient emperors who had lived for a thousand years, and the legendary Princess of Yamato, who had reached the age of five hundred.

Edward's mind turned to the Chinese King, Shin-no-Shiko, who had built the Great Wall of China and lived a life of luxury, yet was consumed by the thought of death. Ah, if only he could find the Elixir of Life, he would be happy. The King had asked his courtiers to find the magical draught, and one old sage, Jofuku, had been sent to the land of Horaizan, where hermits possessed the secret. Though Jofuku never returned, the people believed that Mount Fuji was the fabled Horaizan, and he was revered as a patron saint.

Edward, determined to find the hermits, set out on his quest, leaving his relatives to manage his estate. He traveled through the mountainous regions, climbing to the highest peaks, but found no hermits. A kindly hunter, who seemed to sense Edward's purpose, offered no guidance, but spoke of a notorious robber who led a band of two hundred followers. Frustrated, Edward decided to visit the shrine of Jofuku, who was worshiped as the patron god of the hermits in the south.

At the shrine, Jofuku appeared to Edward in a vision, saying, "You are not like ordinary men, for you are idle and sensitive to heat and cold. You would never be able to live as a hermit, nor find the Elixir of Life." In a surprising twist, Jofuku offered Edward a different solution. "I will send you to the Country of Perpetual Life, where death never comes, and the people live forever!"

Jofuku handed Edward a small paper crane, instructing him to sit on its back, and it would carry him to the fabled land. Edward obeyed, and the crane grew large enough for him to ride comfortably. It spread its wings and flew over the mountains, out to sea, carrying Edward on a fantastical journey. After several days, they reached an island, and the crane alighted. Edward was amazed, for he had not felt hunger or thirst during the journey.

As he explored the island, Edward discovered that the inhabitants were unlike anyone he had ever met. They were prosperous, yet longed for death, believing it would lead them to the Paradise of contentment, which priests from India and China had described to them. The people consumed "poisons" in the hope of dying, but these substances had no effect on them. Edward realized that everything in this land was upside down, and he, who had once feared death, now found himself in a place where people sought it.

As the years passed, Edward's experiences in the Country of Perpetual Life became increasingly monotonous. He faced business losses, and conflicts with his neighbors, which filled him with annoyance. He longed to return to his own land, to his family and home, and to die a natural death. In his desperation, he prayed to Jofuku, who, in answer to his plea, sent the paper crane to carry him back.

However, as Edward looked back at the land he had left, he regretted his decision. He tried to stop the crane, but it flew on, carrying him across the ocean. A storm arose, and the crane crumpled, falling into the sea. Edward was swept away, struggling to stay afloat, and a monstrous shark approached him. In his terror, he cried out to Jofuku, and as he did, he awoke to find himself back at the shrine, having fallen asleep during his prayer.

A messenger stood before him, holding a book. "I am sent by Jofuku," he said. "You have been granted a vision of the Country of Perpetual Life, but your desire for death was not genuine. You have been tested, and it is time for you to return to your own land and live a good and industrious life. Never neglect to honor your ancestors, and provide for your children's future. Thus, you will live to a good old age and be happy."

Edward, chastened by his experience, took the book to heart and returned to his family home, determined to live a virtuous life and follow the precepts contained in the book. And so, he and his household prospered, free from the vain desires that had led him on a fantastical journey.
