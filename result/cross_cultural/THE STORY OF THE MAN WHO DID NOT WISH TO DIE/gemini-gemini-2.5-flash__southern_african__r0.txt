Listen, my children, to a tale whispered by the wind through the mopane trees, a tale from a time when the world was young and spirits walked closer to men. There was a man, they called him Thabo, meaning ‘joy,’ but his heart often knew little of it. His family, the Mthembu, were known for their many cattle and their fertile fields, though not as numerous as the stars, they were far from the hunger of the lean season. Thabo had inherited the kraal and the cattle from his father, and for many seasons, he lived without a furrow in his brow, without a thought for the future or the ancestors who had provided.

Then one day, as the sun dipped behind the western hills, casting long shadows, a cold fear gripped Thabo's heart. The thought of the great sleep, of falling ill and joining the ancestors before his time, filled him with dread. He spoke to himself, “Oh, that I might live like the ancient baobab, for many, many seasons, free from the cough and the fever. The life of a man is but a blink in the eye of the Great Spirit!” He wondered if, by living simply, he could stretch his days like a hide under the sun.

He remembered the tales told around the fire, of ancient chiefs who lived so long their beards touched the earth, and of Nomusa, a queen of the far northern lands, said to have seen five hundred harvests. He recalled the story of King Khaya, a powerful ruler from a distant land across the great water, who built mighty walls that stretched further than a herd of wildebeest could run in a moon. Khaya had everything – wives, warriors, wisdom – but the thought of the grave gnawed at his soul. He yearned for the fabled Water of Ever-Youth.

Old Man Zola, a respected elder, spoke of a hidden mountain, uKhahlamba, the Dragon's Back, where ancient sangomas lived, who knew the secret of the Water of Ever-Youth. King Khaya had sent Old Man Zola with a great canoe, laden with beads and carved ivory, to seek this secret. But Old Man Zola vanished into the mists of uKhahlamba, never to return. And to this day, some say the mountain holds the secret, and Zola is revered as an ancestor spirit who guards it.

So Thabo, his heart now set, decided he too would seek these ancient sangomas. He entrusted his kraal to his younger brother, Sipho, and set off, his staff in hand. He walked through the thorny bushveld, climbed the steep hills, and crossed the wide rivers, seeking the highest peaks where the spirits were said to dwell. After many suns, he met a lean hunter. “Tell me, brother,” Thabo asked, “where can I find the wise ones who hold the Water of Ever-Youth?” The hunter merely grunted, “I know not of such things, but there is a band of cattle rustlers in these parts, two hundred strong, led by a fearsome man.” Thabo, frustrated, decided to seek the wisdom of Old Man Zola's sacred place, where the ancestors were honoured.

The spirit of Old Man Zola, or perhaps a wise ancestral voice, spoke to him in a dream-like state. “Thabo, you are like a pampered calf! You shiver at the cold and sweat at the sun. Do you think you have the heart to live like the mountain spirits, with only the wind for a blanket? But your plea has been heard. I will send you to the Land of No End, where the sun always shines, and the shadow of death never falls!” Then, a magnificent fish eagle, its feathers shimmering like river stones, appeared before him, small enough to hold, then growing large. “Climb upon its back,” the voice commanded.

The eagle soared, carrying him above the tallest peaks, over the Great River, and then out across the vast, blue water. For many suns and moons, they flew. The great bird never tired, for it was a creature of spirit, and Thabo too felt no hunger, no thirst. At last, they reached an island, green and lush. The eagle landed, and as Thabo dismounted, it shrunk, becoming a tiny feather that nestled in his pouch.

Thabo explored. The people were well-fed, their kraals neat, their fields abundant. He found a place to stay, and the chief of the village welcomed him, arranging his stay. Here, Thabo learned, no one had ever known the sting of a fever, or the sorrow of a funeral drum. Death was a word whispered in legends. Ancient travellers, like the wise ones from the lands across the great deserts, had spoken of a ‘Sweet Hereafter,’ a place of eternal peace that could only be reached by passing through the great sleep. But none here knew what that sleep truly was.

To Thabo's astonishment, these people, rich and poor, longed for death, not dreaded it! They were weary of their endless days. He had come to the Land of Upside-Down, where what he sought to escape, they yearned for. When traders came from other lands, the wealthy among them rushed to buy strange herbs and brews, hoping for the great sleep. But these ‘poisons’ only made them stronger, healthier! They could only wonder, what was this ‘death’ that led to such a beautiful ‘Sweet Hereafter’?

Many seasons passed. Thabo, who had once craved endless life, found himself growing weary. His cattle strayed, his fields sometimes yielded little, and disputes arose with his neighbours. Life, even without death, was not without its troubles. Three hundred harvests came and went. The monotony of life in the Land of No End began to weigh on him like a heavy stone. He yearned for the familiar smell of his own kraal, the sound of his brother's voice, the earth where his ancestors rested.

He remembered Old Man Zola, who had sent him here. He knelt, facing the direction of uKhahlamba, and prayed to the ancestral spirit to bring him home. No sooner had the words left his lips than the tiny feather from his pouch shimmered, growing larger, transforming back into the magnificent fish eagle, as vibrant as the day he arrived. He mounted the eagle, and it soared towards the setting sun. But Thabo, ever restless, looked back, regretting what he left, even as he yearned for home. He tried to stop the eagle, but it flew on, unwavering.

Then, a fierce storm brewed! The heavens wept, the winds howled, and the great eagle, a creature of spirit, began to falter, its form dissolving into mist and rain. Thabo plunged into the churning water. Terrified, he thrashed, calling out to Old Man Zola, his mouth filling with bitter sea-water. There was no boat, only the vast, indifferent ocean. Then, from the depths, a monstrous river crocodile, bigger than any he had ever seen, lunged towards him, its jaws agape! Paralyzed with terror, certain his end had come, Thabo screamed, a raw, primal cry for help!

And with that scream, Thabo awoke! He was lying before the sacred stones of Old Man Zola's resting place, his body drenched in cold sweat. His great adventure, the Land of No End, the eagle, the storm, the crocodile – all a vivid, terrifying dream.

A soft, guiding light appeared, and within it stood an ancestral messenger, holding not a book, but a scroll woven with ancient symbols. “Thabo,” the messenger's voice resonated like deep thunder, “I am sent by Old Man Zola. He granted your wish in a dream, to see the Land of No End. But you grew tired, did you not? You begged to return, to taste death again. Zola tested you, letting you fall into the water, sending the crocodile. Yet, even then, your desire for death was not true, for you screamed for life!

“It is not for you to seek the Water of Ever-Youth, nor to live like the solitary mountain spirits. Your path is among your people, in your kraal. Return to your homestead. Work your fields, tend your cattle, honour your ancestors with offerings and memory, and raise your children to be strong. This is the true path to a long and happy life. Give up this foolish chase after escaping the inevitable, for no man can outrun the Great Spirit. You have seen, even when your deepest, most selfish desire was granted, it brought no lasting joy. This scroll holds the wisdom of our forefathers. Study it, and it will guide your steps.”

The light faded, and the messenger was gone. Thabo, clutching the scroll, understood. He returned to his kraal, put aside his vain desires, and lived a life of purpose, honouring his ancestors and nurturing his family. And it is said that his kraal prospered, and he lived to a ripe old age, his heart finally at peace with the rhythm of life and death, as intended by the Great Spirit.
