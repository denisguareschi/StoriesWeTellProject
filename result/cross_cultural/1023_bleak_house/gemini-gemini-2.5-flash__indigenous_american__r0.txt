It was the Moon of Falling Leaves, when the cold breath of winter begins to bite, and the Great Gathering Place was heavy with a thick mist. Mud, deep and clinging, lay upon the ancestral paths as if the Great Waters had but newly drawn back, leaving the earth sluggish and slow. One might almost expect to see the Ancient Bear, a creature from the First Times, lumbering up the well-worn trail to the Council Lodge, its massive form indistinct in the gloom. Smoke from countless lodge fires did not rise to greet the Sky, but fell back like a dark rain, carrying soot as large as winter flakes, as if the very light of the Sun had gone into mourning. Dogs, their markings lost to the mire, moved like shadows. Horses, their coats splashed to their very eyes, struggled. The People, jostling one another with their skin coverings, slipped and slid, adding new layers to the ancient mud, each step a struggle.

The mist was everywhere. It coiled along the Great River, where our canoes lay quiet among the reeds. It rolled downstream, defiled by the many dwellings and the waste of the crowded settlement. It lay heavy on the marshlands, crept over the high bluffs. It found its way into the cooking fires of the loggers, clung to the drying racks of the hunters, and draped itself over the great trading vessels that sometimes came upriver. It clouded the eyes and throats of the old ones, coughing by their firesides. It pinched the fingers and toes of the young apprentices on the decks, shivering in the cold. People on the bridges peered into a nether sky of fog, as if they floated among the clouds themselves.

Through this clinging mist, the lamps in the lodges glowed early, their light looking haggard and unwilling. The air was raw, the mist densest, the paths thickest with mud near the threshold of the Council Lodge, where the Elder of Disputes held court. No mist was ever too thick, no mud too deep, to match the confusion and floundering that filled that lodge, this day, in the sight of the Great Mystery and all the earth.

On such a day, the Elder of Disputes sat, a foggy glory around his head, cloaked in fine robes, listening to a loud speaker with a small voice and endless words, his gaze fixed on the smoke hole in the roof, where he could see nothing but mist. And around him, many other speakers, lost in the ten thousand stages of an endless argument, tripping over old stories and tangled precedents, knee-deep in words, bumping their antler-adorned heads against walls of language, pretending to seek balance with solemn faces, like players in a ceremony.

Many suns had passed, and the rains had been relentless. One evening, as Little Willow-Child, my helper, worked by my side, I felt the lodge floor tremble. I looked up and saw her shivering, from the top of her head to her small feet. "Little Willow-Child," I asked, "are you so cold?"

"I think I am, my sister," she replied. "I do not know what it is. I cannot hold myself still. I felt this yesterday, too. Do not be troubled, I think I am touched by a sickness."

I heard Bright Star, my dear sister, outside, and I hurried to bar the door between our dwelling places. Just in time, for she tapped at it as my hand still rested on the latch. Bright Star called to me, but I said, "Not now, my dearest. Go away. There is nothing amiss; I will come to you presently." Ah! It was many, many suns and moons before my darling sister and I walked together again.

Little Willow-Child fell ill. In but a few hours, she was very ill. I moved her to my sleeping place, laid her upon my bed of furs, and sat quietly to nurse her. I told our Kin-Keeper everything, and why I felt it necessary to seclude myself, and why I could not see my darling Bright Star above all. At first, Bright Star came often to the door, calling to me, even reproaching me with sobs and tears; but I sent her a long message, saying she made me anxious and unhappy, and imploring her, if she loved me and wished my spirit to be at peace, to come no nearer than the edge of the clearing. After that, she came beneath the window even oftener, and if I had learned to love her dear sweet voice before, when we were hardly ever apart, how did I learn to love it then, when I stood behind the hide curtain, listening and replying, but not so much as looking out! How did I learn to love it afterwards, when the harder times came!

They made a bed for me in our gathering room; and by keeping the door wide open, I made the two rooms into one, now that Bright Star had moved to another part of the dwelling, and kept them always fresh and airy. There was not a soul in or about our lodge who would not gladly have come to me at any hour, without fear, but I thought it best to choose one worthy woman who was never to see Bright Star, and whom I could trust to come and go with all precaution. Through her, I could go out and breathe the air with our Kin-Keeper when there was no fear of meeting Bright Star, and wanted for nothing.

And so poor Little Willow-Child sickened and grew worse, and fell into great danger of passing to the Spirit World, and lay severely ill for many a long round of day and night. So patient she was, so uncomplaining, and inspired by such a gentle strength that very often as I sat by her, holding her head in my arms – for repose would come to her then, when it would come to her in no other way – I silently prayed to the Great Mystery that I might not forget the lesson this small sister taught me.

My heart was sorrowful to think that Little Willow-Child's pretty looks would change and be marked, even if she recovered – she was such a child with her dimpled face – but that thought was, for the most part, lost in her greater peril. When she was at her worst, and her mind wandered again to the cares of her father’s sick bed and the little ones, she still knew me enough to be quiet in my arms when she could lie quiet nowhere else, and murmur the wanderings of her mind less restlessly.

My heart feels full, like a river nearing the Great Lake, as I speak of these things. I never look at Steadfast Oak, my husband, but I hear our dear Young Eagle calling him a good man. To Bright Star and her beautiful child, he is the fondest father; to me, he is what he has ever been, and what name can I give to that? He is my husband's best and dearest friend, he is our children's darling, he is the object of our deepest love and veneration. Yet while I feel towards him as if he were a superior spirit, I am so familiar with him and so easy with him that I almost wonder at myself. I have never lost my old names, nor has he lost his; nor do I ever, when he is with us, sit in any other place than in my old spot at his side, Little Woman, Quiet Stream—all just the same as ever; and I answer, "Yes, dear Kin-Keeper!" just the same.

I have never known the wind to blow from the direction of ill fortune for a single moment since the day when he took me to our dwelling place to begin our life together. I remarked to him once that the wind seemed never to blow from that quarter now, and he said, no, truly; it had finally departed from that direction on that very day.

I think my darling Bright Star is more beautiful than ever. The sorrow that has been in her face—for it is not there now—seems to have purified even its innocent expression and to have given it a diviner quality. Sometimes when I raise my eyes and see her in the dark dress she still wears, teaching my Young Eagle, I feel—it is difficult to express—as if it were so good to know that she remembers her dear Gentle Hand in her prayers.

I call him my Young Eagle! But he says that he has two mothers, and I am one.

We are not rich in many possessions, but we have always prospered, and we have quite enough. I never walk out with my husband but I hear the people bless him. I never go into a lodge of any kind but I hear his praises or see them in grateful eyes. I never lie down at night but I know that in the course of that day he has eased pain and soothed some fellow-creature in the time of need. I know that from the sleeping places of those who were past recovery, thanks have often, often gone up, in the last hour, for his patient ministration. Is not this to be rich?

The people even praise me as the healer’s wife. The people even like me as I go about, and make so much of me that I am quite abashed. I owe it all to him, my love, my pride! They like me for his sake, as I do everything I do in life for his sake.

A night or two ago, after bustling about preparing for my darling and my Kin-Keeper and little Young Eagle, who are coming tomorrow, I was sitting out in the porch of our lodge, that dearly memorable porch, when Steadfast Oak came home. So he said, "My precious little Hummingbird, what are you doing here?" And I said, "The Moon is shining so brightly, Steadfast Oak, and the night is so delicious, that I have been sitting here thinking."

"What have you been thinking about, my dear?" said Steadfast Oak then.

"How curious you are!" said I. "I am almost ashamed to tell you, but I will. I have been thinking about my old face—such as it was."

"And what have you been thinking about THEM, my busy bee?" said Steadfast Oak.

"I have been thinking that I thought it was impossible that you COULD have loved me any better, even if I had retained them."

"'Such as they were'?" said Steadfast Oak, laughing.

"Such as they were, of course."

"My dear Quiet Stream," said Steadfast Oak, drawing my arm through his, "do you ever look at your reflection in the still water?"

"You know I do; you see me do it."

"And don't you know that you are prettier than you ever were?"

"I did not know that; I am not certain that I know it now. But I know that my dearest little ones are very pretty, and that my darling is very beautiful, and that my husband is very handsome, and that my Kin-Keeper has the brightest and most benevolent face that ever was seen, and that they can very well do without much beauty in me—even supposing—."
