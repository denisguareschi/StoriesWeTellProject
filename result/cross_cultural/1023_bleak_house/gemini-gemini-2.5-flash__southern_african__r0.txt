The season of the heavy mists had come, cloaking the Great *Kgotla* where the elders gathered to hear disputes. The paths to the council place were churned to a thick, clinging mud, as if the Great River had only just retreated from the face of the earth, and one might expect to see the ancient beasts of the first sun, lumbering through the mire. Smoke from a thousand cooking fires mingled with the morning chill, weeping black tears, as if the sky itself mourned the hidden sun. Dogs, their coats caked with mud, were indistinguishable from the earth. Cattle, their hooves heavy, struggled through the clinging soil. People, wrapped in their blankets, bumped shoulders, their tempers as sharp as winter thorns, losing their footing where generations had slipped before, adding new layers to the ancient crust of mud.

The mist, *thokgamo*, lay heavy over all. It crept from the great river, winding through the green reeds and cattle pastures. It rolled down from the distant mountains, cloaking the homesteads. It clung to the hides of the herd boys’ cattle, it seeped into the very bones of the old ones gathered by the fire, making their chests rattle like dry gourds. The flickering light of the *kgotla* fire, usually a beacon, now merely loomed through the oppressive veil.

And there, at the very heart of the mist, beneath the ancient shade of the Great Ancestor Tree, sat *Kgosi* Thabiso, the Great Chief, presiding over the endless dispute. No mist could be thick enough, no mud deep enough, to mirror the tangled, churning confusion of the Great Cattle Dispute of the Many Rivers, a sickness that had festered in the heart of the people for generations, seen by the ancestors and the spirits of the land. On such a day, the *Kgosi* sat, a foggy glory around his head, listening to a respected orator, his voice thin as dry grass, his words a river that never reached the sea. They tripped each other with ancient rulings, waded knee-deep in the tangled roots of the law, their minds like goats butting against a stone wall of words, pretending wisdom with solemn faces, like children playing a serious game.

***

The rains had been relentless, soaking the earth, washing away all tracks. For five sunrises and five sunsets, the searchers combed the bush, the cattle kraals, the woven fences, but young *Thando* had vanished like a shadow at noon.

One evening, as my young helper, *Lerato*, was weaving a mat by the fire in my hut, I felt the ground beneath my feet tremble, a shiver ran through the hut. Looking up, I saw Lerato shivering from head to foot, her teeth chattering like dry seeds in a gourd.

“Lerato,” I said, “are you cold?”

“My spirit feels cold, Nomusa,” she replied. “I don’t know what it is. I cannot hold myself still. I felt this yesterday, at this same hour. Do not be uneasy, I think I am ill.”

*Zola’s* voice called from outside. I hurried to the entrance of my hut, pulling the reed mat tight, securing it with a swift hand. Just in time, for she tapped at it even as my fingers held the thong.

Zola called to me to let her in, but I said, “Not now, my beloved sister. Go. There is nothing amiss; I will come to you presently.” Ah! It was a long, long time before my darling Zola and I were companions again.

Lerato fell into a deep sickness. Before the next sun had climbed high, her fever burned like a veld fire. I moved her to my own sleeping mat, laying her gently, and sat by her side to nurse her, my heart heavy like a stone. I explained to *Mkhulu* Jabulani why I must keep myself apart, why I could not allow my beloved Zola to come near. At first, she came often to my hut entrance, calling to me, even reproaching me with sobs and tears. But I sent her a message through a trusted elder, a long message, begging her, for the love she bore me and for the peace of my spirit, to keep her distance, to stay by the cattle path, not to come closer than the edge of the homestead. After that, she came to the edge of the fence, beneath the shade of the Marula tree, even more often than she had come to the door. And if I had learned to love her dear, sweet voice before, when we were hardly ever apart, how deeply did I learn to love it then, when I stood behind the screen of woven reeds, listening and replying, but never showing my face! How much more did I learn to love it afterwards, when the true shadow of the illness fell!

They made a sleeping place for me in the larger hut, and by keeping the entrance wide, the two huts became one, now that Zola was not there, and I kept them always fresh and airy. Every servant in the homestead was good, and would have come to me at any hour, without fear or unwillingness. But I thought it best to choose one trusted woman, discreet as a night bird, who would never cross paths with Zola, and whom I could trust to come and go with all precaution. Through her means, I walked with Mkhulu Jabulani in the cool of the evening, when there was no chance of meeting Zola, and lacked for nothing.

And so, young Lerato sickened, her breath growing shallow, her spirit hovering between this world and the world of the ancestors. So patient she was, so uncomplaining, inspired by a gentle fortitude that very often, as I sat by Lerato, holding her head in my arms—repose would come to her then, when it would come to her in no other attitude—I silently prayed to the Great Spirit, to the ancestors, that I might never forget the lesson this small sister taught me.

I was sorrowful to think that Lerato’s bright face, her dimpled cheeks, might be marked forever, even if she survived this ordeal. But that thought was, for the greater part, lost in her greater peril. When she was at her worst, and her mind wandered again to the worries of her own family, the illness of her father, the cries of her younger siblings, still, she knew me, and found peace in my arms when no other comfort could reach her, and murmured the wanderings of her mind less restlessly.

***

I try to tell this tale with a light heart, for the journey nears its end, but when I speak of him, the tears of joy and memory well in my eyes.

I never look at him but I hear our dear *Sizwe* calling him a man of true spirit. To Zola and her bright-eyed son, he is the fondest father; to me he is what he has ever been, and what name can I give to that? He is my husband’s brother of the heart, our children’s beloved elder, he is the object of our deepest love and veneration. Yet while I feel towards him as if he were a mighty tree, I am so familiar with him and so easy with him that I almost wonder at myself. I have never lost my old names, nor has he lost his; nor do I ever, when he is with us, sit in any other place than in my old mat at his side. *Nomusa*, *Mma-Sizwe*, Little Woman—all just the same as ever; and I answer, “Yes, Mkhulu Jabulani, my wise guide!” just the same.

I have never known the wind of sorrow to blow from the dry lands for a single moment since the day when he took me to the entrance of my hut to read the name. I remarked to him once that the wind seemed never from the east now, and he said, no, truly; it had finally departed from that quarter on that very day.

I think my beloved Zola glows brighter than ever. The shadows that once touched her face—for they are gone now—seem to have deepened the grace of her spirit, like the dawn breaking after a long night. Sometimes when I raise my eyes and see her in the dark cloth she still wears, teaching my young Sizwe, I feel—it is difficult to express—as if it were so good to know that she remembers her dear Nomusa in her thoughts.

I call him my Sizwe! But he declares he has two mothers, and I am one of them.

We have no great kraal of cattle, no overflowing granary of millet, but the blessings of the ancestors have followed us, and we have always had enough, and more. I never walk out with my husband but I hear the people speak his praise-names. I never go into a homestead of any degree but I hear his praises or see them in grateful eyes. I never lie down at night but I know that in the course of that day he has eased the pain, soothed the troubled spirit, healed the sick in their hour of need. I know that from the mats of those whose journey to the ancestors was near, thanks have risen, again and again, for his gentle hand, his patient spirit. Is this not wealth beyond measure?

The people even speak my praise-names, calling me the healer’s wife. The people even like me as I go about, and honour me so, I feel my cheeks flush with shyness. All this, I owe to him, my heart’s companion, my strength! They like me for his sake, as I do everything I do in life for his sake.

A night or two ago, after stirring about, preparing food and sleeping mats for my beloved Zola, for Mkhulu Jabulani, and for young Sizwe, who are coming tomorrow, I was sitting by the entrance of my hut, in that very spot, so dear to my heart, when *Dumisani* came home. So he said, “My precious little reed, what keeps you here, beneath the moon?” And I said, “The moon, *Naledi*, shines so bright, Dumisani, and the night air is sweet like honey, that I have been sitting here, letting my thoughts wander.”

“What thoughts dance in your head, my heart?” said Dumisani then.

“Ah, you are a curious one!” said I. “I am almost ashamed to tell you, but I will. I have been thinking about my old face—as it once was.”

“And what have you been thinking about *that*, my busy bee?” said Dumisani.

“I have been thinking that I thought it was impossible that you could have loved me any better, even if I had retained it.”

“‘As it once was’?” said Dumisani, laughing.

“As it once was, of course.”

“My dear little ant,” said Dumisani, drawing my arm through his, “do you ever gaze into the still pool of water?”

“You know I do; you see me sometimes, by the river’s edge.”

“And do you not know, my Nomusa, that your beauty now shines brighter than it ever did before?”

“I did not know that; I am not certain I know it now. But I know that my little ones are bright as morning stars, that my Zola is beautiful as the dawn, that my Dumisani is handsome as a young bull, and that Mkhulu Jabulani has the kindest, most radiant face in all the lands. And truly, they can do very well without much beauty in me—even if I were to think—.”
