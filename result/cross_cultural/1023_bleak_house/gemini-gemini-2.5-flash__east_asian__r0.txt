In the old capital of Edo, as late autumn yielded to the biting winds of early winter, a thick, damp mist settled. The venerable magistrate, Ooka-sama, sat in his grand hall, the air heavy with damp earth and hearth smoke. Streets were a quagmire, as if the Sumida River had but lately receded. Soot, black as a raven's wing, drifted from tiled roofs, mingling with the mist, as if the very sky mourned the sun. Dogs and palanquins were indistinguishable in the mire; foot-travelers jostled with oiled-paper umbrellas, slipping on slick stones, adding layer upon layer to the ancient mud. Mist clung everywhere: from reedy river islets to bustling docks, veiling distant hills. Lanterns, paper and iron, glowed with a haggard, unwilling light through the shrouding fog, lit hours before their time. The raw afternoon was rawest, the mist densest, the muddy streets most treacherous near the great Sakurada Gate. And within the heart of this mist, in the hall of justice, sat Ooka-sama, the High Magistrate, amidst scholars and petitioners, their endless cases as tangled and opaque as the fog itself.

The weeks that followed brought quiet worry. My young attendant, Saki, had vanished one evening, only to reappear five days later, shivering uncontrollably. "I fear I am unwell, Haru-sama," she whispered. Hearing Ayame’s voice outside, I quickly secured the sliding screen. "Not now, dearest Ayame," I replied. Ah, it was long before my precious friend and I could share companionship again. Saki’s illness worsened swiftly. I moved her to my own chamber, laying her in my futon, settling into quiet nursing. I explained to our revered Oyakata-sama why I must seclude myself from all, especially Ayame. Though Ayame often called from outside, I implored her, for my peace, to keep her distance. I cherished her sweet voice, listening from behind the paper screen, never looking out. Poor Saki grew worse, hovering at the edge of life for many a long day and night. So patient, so uncomplaining, her gentle fortitude was a quiet lesson. Often, holding her head, I prayed to Kannon, Bodhisattva of Mercy, never to forget. I grieved her pretty face might be disfigured, but that sorrow was swallowed by her greater peril. Even when her mind wandered, she found solace in my arms, her murmurs softening.

My heart swells with emotion as I draw to a close. Every time I look at him, I hear our dear departed Ren calling him a good man. To Ayame and her son, he is the most loving father; to me, he is what he has always been, a beloved husband and our children’s delight. I have not known the wind to blow from the east since the day he led me to the *engawa* to read the name. He said, truly, it had departed from that quarter on that very day. We are not rich in coin, but we prosper. I never walk with my Kenji but I hear people bless him; I never lie down at night but I know he has eased pain and comforted a soul. Is this not true wealth?

A night or two ago, sitting on our *engawa*, Kenji came home. "My precious little blossom," he said, "what are you doing here?" I replied, "The moon shines so brightly, Kenji, I was thinking about my old appearance – such as it was."
"'Such as it was'?" Kenji laughed softly. "My dear Haru, do you ever look in the mirror? Don't you know you are more beautiful than you ever were?"
"I did not know that," I said. "But I know my little ones are pretty, Ayame is beautiful, my husband is handsome, and Oyakata-sama has the brightest, most benevolent face. They can very well do without much beauty in me – even supposing–."
