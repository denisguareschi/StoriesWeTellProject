The London air, thick with the damp chill of a late November, hung heavy over the city. Michaelmas term had but lately concluded, yet the Lord Chancellor remained, presiding over his court in Lincoln's Inn Hall. Such was the season’s gloom, the very streets seemed to weep, a mire of mud so profound it appeared the earth had only just emerged from a primal flood. Smoke, a funereal pall, drifted from a thousand chimney-pots, settling as a soft, black drizzle, its flakes of soot large as winter’s first snow. Dogs and horses alike were indistinguishable beneath their coatings of filth, while pedestrians, a mass of jostling umbrellas and ill-temper, slipped and slid on the greasy pavements, adding new layers to the accumulating grime.

Everywhere, fog. It coiled along the river, where it crept amongst the green islets and meadows, and rolled, defiled, through the shipping lanes of the great, grimy city. It stretched across the Essex marshes and clung to the Kentish heights, seeping into the cabooses of collier-brigs, shrouding the yards of great ships, and drooping heavily on the gunwales of small boats. It filled the eyes and throats of aged Greenwich pensioners, wheezing by their ward firesides, and pinched the toes of shivering apprentice boys on deck. This pervasive, leaden mist enveloped all, making the gas lamps loom like spectral moons and the shops light their windows hours before their time, their glow a haggard, unwilling defiance against the encroaching murk. At the very heart of this impenetrable gloom, in his High Court of Chancery, sat the Lord High Chancellor, a figure of foggy glory, overseeing the endless, floundering condition of that most pestilent institution, a monument to legal stagnation.

Some time later, after a fruitless search for the missing boy, a search that had spanned five days and yielded nothing but the damp silence of the countryside, my attention was drawn away by a matter far more personal. I sat opposite my little maid, Charley, as she diligently worked at her writing in my room one evening, when I felt the table tremble. Looking up, I saw her shivering uncontrollably. “Charley,” I enquired, “are you so cold?”

“I believe I am, miss,” she replied, her voice faint. “I cannot hold myself still. I felt this same way yesterday. I fear I am unwell.”

Hearing Ada’s voice outside, I rushed to the communicating door and locked it just as she tapped. “Not now, my dearest,” I called, forcing a cheerfulness I did not feel. “Go away. I will come to you presently.” It was a long, sorrowful time before my darling girl and I were companions again.

Charley’s illness was swift and severe. Within twelve hours, she was gravely sick. I moved her to my own bed and began to nurse her, having explained to my guardian the necessity of my seclusion, particularly from Ada. Though Ada often came to my window, calling and even weeping, I wrote to her, imploring her, for my peace of mind, to keep her distance. I prayed often by Charley’s bedside, holding her dear head, that I might never forget the lesson of patience and gentle fortitude this little sister taught me, even as I grieved the thought of her pretty looks disfigured by the disease.

Now, as I draw these reflections to a close, my heart swells, and my tears, when I think of him, will have their way. My dearest Allan, he is to Ada and her boy the fondest father; to me, he is what he has ever been, a love beyond name. My husband, our children's darling, the object of our deepest veneration. Yet, despite holding him almost as a superior being, I am so familiar, so easy with him, I marvel at myself. My old names – Dame Trot, Dame Durden, Little Woman – he still uses, and I still answer, "Yes, dear guardian!" just the same.

The wind, I’ve noticed, has never been in the east since the day he took me to the porch to read the name. He confirmed it, saying it had truly departed that very day. My darling girl, Ada, is more beautiful than ever, her sorrow-purified face possessing a diviner quality. And though we are not rich in the bank, we have always prospered, having quite enough. I never walk out with Allan but I hear the people bless him, never enter a house but I hear his praises or see them in grateful eyes. I know that daily he alleviates pain, soothes distress, and for his patient ministration, thanks rise often from beds of the past recovery. Is not this to be rich?

The people even praise me as the doctor’s wife, making so much of me that I am quite abashed. I owe it all to him, my love, my pride! They like me for his sake, as I do everything in life for his sake. A night or two ago, after preparing for their arrival, I sat in that dearly memorable porch when Allan came home. "My precious little woman," he said, "what are you doing here?" I explained I was thinking, gazing at the moon. "What about, my dear?" he asked. "My old looks," I confessed, "such as they were." He laughed, drawing my arm through his. "My dear Dame Durden," he said, "do you ever look in the glass? Don't you know you are prettier than you ever were?" I did not know, nor am I certain now, but I know my pets are pretty, my darling beautiful, my husband handsome, and my guardian possesses the brightest, most benevolent face ever seen. And they, I think, can very well do without much beauty in me.
