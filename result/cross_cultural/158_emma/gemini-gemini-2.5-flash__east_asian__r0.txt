In the heart of Matsuoka-mura, a village nestled among ancient pines and whispering bamboo, resided Emiko of the Morioka household. Blessed with beauty, sharp wit, and a fortunate birthright, her spirit shone like a spring blossom, seeming to unite all the best blessings under the sun. For twenty-one years, she had known little of hardship or vexation.

As the younger daughter of Lord Morioka, a father of boundless affection and indulgence, Emiko-hime had, since her elder sister’s marriage, taken the reins of their grand *yashiki* at a tender age. Her mother’s memory was but a faint, cherished fragrance from long ago, her place filled by Tayoko-sensei, a woman whose gentle heart and devoted care had been as a second mother. For sixteen years, Tayoko-sensei had graced the Morioka home, less a tutor and more a beloved companion, particularly to Emiko. Their bond was akin to sisters, a shared intimacy where the tutor’s mild temper had long softened the shadow of authority, leaving Emiko to follow the dictates of her own bright mind, though always valuing Tayoko-sensei’s counsel.

Yet, the true shadow threatening Emiko’s radiant joy lay in her spirit’s very brightness – a tendency to have too much her own way, and to think a little too well of her own judgments. These were the subtle currents that could stir the calm waters of her many delights, though for now, she perceived them not as flaws but as facets of her unique self.

Then came a sorrow, gentle as a falling autumn leaf, but profound. Tayoko-sensei was to marry Nishimura-san. It was the parting from this cherished friend that first brought a true ache to Emiko’s heart. On the wedding day, as the guests departed and the joyful music faded, Emiko and her father were left to dine in the vast *yashiki*, the evening stretching long and quiet. Her father, as was his custom, soon drifted into a light slumber, leaving Emiko to ponder the void in her days. Though her friend found happiness, Emiko felt a deep, personal loss, recalling Tayoko-sensei’s kindness, her patient guidance, her unwavering affection through sixteen years. The comfort of their shared existence, the perfect understanding that had grown since her sister’s own marriage, was a precious treasure now diminished. Though Okusama Nishimura would reside but half a *ri* away, Emiko knew the profound difference between a friend at one’s side and one across the village. She dearly loved her father, but his gentle spirit could not fill the intellectual solitude that now threatened her.

“Oh, Furuhata-sama! There is nothing like it!”

“Indeed, it was delightful! I could say no less, knowing that Emiko-hime and Furuhata-sama might hear every word. And,” Kageyama-dono raised his voice, a hint of playful gruffness in his tone, “let us not forget O-Junko-san. Her dancing is truly exceptional! And Okusama Nishimura, without peer, plays the *shamisen* for the village dances. If your friends possess any gratitude, they will speak loudly of you and me in return; but I cannot linger to hear it.”

“Oh, Kageyama-dono, one moment more! Something of great concern – we are so distressed about the winter apples!”

“What misfortune has befallen the apples now?”

“To think you sent us your entire store! You said you had many, and now not one remains. We are truly shocked! The household manager, O-Hodges-san, must be displeased. Tanaka-san’s servant mentioned it. You should not have been so generous, indeed you should not! Ah, he is gone. He never endures thanks. But I thought he would have stayed this time, and it would have been a pity not to have mentioned… Well,” Emiko returned to the room, “I could not succeed. Kageyama-dono cannot stop. He journeys to the castle town of Kōyama. He asked if he could assist in any way…”

“Yes,” said O-Junko-san, “we heard his kind offers, we heard everything.”

“Oh, yes, my dear, I daresay you might, for the *shōji* was open, and the window likewise, and Kageyama-dono spoke loudly. You must have heard every word. ‘Can I do anything for you at Kōyama?’ he asked; so I merely mentioned… Oh, Emiko-hime, must you depart already? You seem but just arrived—so very obliging of you.”

Emiko indeed found it time to return home; the visit had stretched long. Upon consulting their watches, so much of the morning had passed that Okusama Nishimura and her companion could only accompany the two young ladies to the Morioka-yashiki gates before setting off for the Nishimura-bekkaku.

***

One might live without the joy of dancing. Tales are told of young people passing seasons without a single festive gathering, their bodies and spirits unharmed. But once the fleeting delight of rhythmic motion has been tasted, even lightly, only the most somber heart does not yearn for more.

Furuhata-sama had danced once at Matsuoka-mura and longed to dance again. The last half-hour of an evening that Lord Morioka was persuaded to spend with his daughter at Nishimura-bekkaku was filled with schemes between the two young people. Furuhata-sama’s was the initial spark, and his the greatest zeal, for Emiko-hime, ever the discerning hostess, was more mindful of the practicalities and appearances. Yet, her own spirit yearned to once again display the delightful harmony she and Furuhata-sama created in dance—to shine in a way that even O-Junko-san could not surpass—and simply for the pure joy of the dance itself, unburdened by vanity. So she assisted him, first in measuring the room they occupied, to gauge its capacity, and then in taking the dimensions of the other parlor, hoping to discover, despite Nishimura-san’s assurances of their equal size, that it was in fact a little larger.

His first proposal, that the dance begun at the Cole’s gathering be concluded there—that the same company be reassembled, and the same musician engaged—met with ready enthusiasm. Nishimura-san embraced the idea with hearty enjoyment, and Okusama Nishimura most willingly offered to play the *koto* and *shamisen* for as long as they wished to dance. Thus began the delightful task of counting the guests and apportioning the necessary space for each pair.

***

O-Haru-san, increasingly drawn away by her commitments to the Tanaka family, spent less and less time at the Morioka-yashiki, a change Emiko did not regret. The deep intimacy between them naturally began to soften into a calmer goodwill, a gentle shift that felt both right and inevitable.

Before the end of the ninth month, Emiko accompanied O-Haru-san to the village shrine and watched as her hand was given to Tanaka-san, a sight that filled her with such complete satisfaction that no past memories, even those connected to the priest O-Elton-san standing before them, could diminish it. Perhaps, indeed, at that moment, she scarcely saw O-Elton-san as anything but the clergyman whose blessing might next fall upon herself. Tanaka-san and O-Haru-san, the last of the three couples to be betrothed, were the first to marry.

O-Junko-san had already departed Matsuoka-mura, returning to the comforts of her beloved home with the Campbell family. Furuhata-sama and his family were also in the capital, awaiting the eleventh month.

The intervening month was the one Emiko and Kageyama-dono, with cautious hope, had chosen for their own union. They resolved that their marriage should take place while Lord John and his wife Isabella were still at the Morioka-yashiki, allowing for a fortnight’s absence on a journey to the seaside, as planned. Lord John, Isabella, and all their other friends approved. But Lord Morioka—how was he to be persuaded? He, who had never spoken of their marriage except as a distant, almost mythical event.

When first broached on the subject, he became so sorrowful that their hopes almost withered. A second mention, however, caused less pain. He began to perceive the inevitability, that he could not prevent it—a promising ripple in the calm surface of his resignation. Still, he was not content. Indeed, he appeared so distressed that his daughter’s courage faltered. She could not bear to witness his suffering, to imagine him feeling neglected; and though her understanding assented to the assurances of both Kageyama-dono and his brother, that his distress would quickly pass once the event was over, she hesitated—she could not proceed.

In this state of suspense, they found aid, not in a sudden illumination of Lord Morioka’s mind, nor any miraculous change in his delicate constitution, but through the workings of the world around them. One night, Okusama Nishimura’s fowl-house was plundered of all its prized turkeys—clearly by human cunning. Other poultry yards in the vicinity also suffered. Such pilfering was akin to a breach of the *yashiki* itself in Lord Morioka’s anxious mind. He became deeply uneasy, and but for the sense of his son-in-law’s protection, he would have lived in wretched alarm every night. The strength, resolve, and calm presence of the Kageyama brothers commanded his fullest trust. While either of them protected him and his, the Morioka-yashiki felt safe. But Lord John Kageyama had to return to the capital by the end of the first week of the eleventh month.

The result of this distress was that, with a far more willing and cheerful consent than his daughter had ever dared to hope for, Emiko was able to fix her wedding day. Within a month of Tanaka-san and O-Haru-san’s marriage, O-Elton-san was called upon to join the hands of Kageyama-dono and Emiko-hime.

The wedding was much like other weddings where the parties held no taste for ostentation or grand parade; and Okusama Elton, from the details recounted by her husband, deemed it utterly modest, far inferior to her own. “Very little white silk, few lace veils; a most pitiful affair! Selina would stare when she heard of it.” But, despite these perceived deficiencies, the wishes, the hopes, the confidence, and the predictions of the small band of true friends who witnessed the ceremony were fully realized in the perfect happiness of the union.
