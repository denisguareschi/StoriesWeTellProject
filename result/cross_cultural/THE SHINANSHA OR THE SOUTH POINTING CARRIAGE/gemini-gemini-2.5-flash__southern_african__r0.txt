Hark, my children, gather close, let the fire warm your spirits and the stories fill your minds. For generations, our elders have spoken of wonders, but few are as curious as the tale of Kgosi Thabo, the wise chief, and his magnificent *Isiqondisi-Mzansi* – the South-Pointer.

Long, long ago, when the world was younger and magic still walked openly amongst men, there lived a great chief named Jabulani. His wife, a woman of deep wisdom, had a vision before their son was born. She saw that her child would be a leader like no other. One warm evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, painting the veld in hues of burnt orange and purple, she walked out to breathe the cool air. She gazed at the vast, star-strewn blanket above, and as her eyes found the bright star that watches over the northern sky, it pulsed with a blinding light, like lightning striking from a clear sky. Soon after, her son, Thabo, entered the world, marked for greatness.

Thabo grew, strong and thoughtful, eventually taking up the mantle of chief from his father. But his early reign was troubled by a dark force: Zwilakhe, the rebel, whose heart was as cold as a winter morning and his head as hard as the granite kopjes. Zwilakhe was a *sangoma* of dark arts, weaving spells of confusion and fear, and no warrior seemed able to defeat him. He sought to tear the land apart and rule it himself, and many were the skirmishes and battles.

Finally, Kgosi Thabo led his impis to confront Zwilakhe on the wide, open plains. Thabo’s warriors charged, their spears glinting like morning dew. But Zwilakhe, with a guttural chant, called forth a mist so dense it swallowed the sun, a fog that coiled and clung, blinding Thabo’s men. In the confusion, Zwilakhe’s forces melted away, laughing at the royal army lost in the white embrace of the sorcerer’s mist. Thabo returned to his great kraal, his heart heavy, pondering how to overcome such trickery.

He spent many days and nights in deep thought, seeking wisdom from the ancestors. At last, a revelation came! He set his finest craftsmen to work, and they built a sturdy wagon, and upon it, carved the figure of a man. This was no ordinary wagon, for the carved man, by some marvel, always pointed to the South, no matter how the wagon turned. This was the *Isiqondisi-Mzansi*, the South-Pointer, and with it, Thabo knew the mists of Zwilakhe would hold no fear.

Again, Kgosi Thabo declared war. He placed the *Isiqondisi-Mzansi* at the head of his army, leading the way. The battle was fierce, and Zwilakhe’s men were pushed back. With a snarl, the rebel chief again conjured his swirling, blinding mist. But this time, Thabo’s warriors stood firm! Following the unwavering finger of the carved man on the *Isiqondisi-Mzansi*, Thabo guided his impis, never faltering, never lost. They pursued Zwilakhe relentlessly, driving his forces towards the Great River, swollen and raging from the season’s heavy rains.

With a final burst of dark magic, Zwilakhe ferried his men across the turbulent waters, taking refuge in a stronghold on the far bank. Thabo arrived, seeing his enemy just out of reach, and his disappointment was a bitter taste. The river was uncrossable, no boats existed then. He ordered his royal tent pitched by the riverbank, his mind wrestling with this new challenge.

One afternoon, lost in thought by a quiet pool, Thabo watched the autumn leaves drift. His gaze settled on a tiny spider, struggling to reach a floating leaf. After many attempts, it succeeded, and the leaf carried it gently across the water to the other side. A spark ignited in Thabo’s mind! If a leaf could carry a spider, surely a larger leaf, shaped and strengthened, could carry a man! He set to work, and with perseverance, crafted the first boat, a simple vessel that danced upon the water. Soon, his men were building many more.

With a fleet of these new *izikebhe*, Kgosi Thabo led his army across the Great River. They stormed Zwilakhe’s stronghold, and this time, there was no escape for the rebel. Thabo achieved a complete victory, bringing peace and prosperity back to his people, who loved him dearly. He continued to invent and innovate, always seeking ways to better their lives.

Many seasons passed, a hundred years, they say. One day, as Kgosi Thabo stood looking up at the sky, it turned a fiery red, and a dazzling light descended, shimmering like gold. As it drew closer, it resolved into a magnificent Sky Serpent, scales gleaming like polished copper. It landed before the chief, bowing its mighty head. His wives and courtiers screamed, fleeing in terror.

But Kgosi Thabo smiled, calling out, “Fear not! This is a messenger from the heavens. My time among you is done!” And with calm dignity, he mounted the great creature. The Sky Serpent began to ascend, rising higher and higher. The wives and courtiers, seeing this, cried, “Wait! We wish to come too!” They rushed forward, some grabbing at its shimmering mane, others at its powerful tail, trying to clamber aboard. But it was not meant for so many. As the Sky Serpent soared, some lost their grip, falling back to the earth, while only a few, including his chief wife, held fast.

The great Sky Serpent carried Kgosi Thabo and a chosen few upwards, until they vanished beyond the clouds, into the realm of the ancestors. Later, his favourite spear and a single, sacred arrow fell from the sky into the royal kraal. They were carefully preserved, a sacred reminder of the wise chief who had walked among them, who had brought the *Isiqondisi-Mzansi* and the boats, and then ascended to join the heavens. And so, the story is told, generation to generation.
